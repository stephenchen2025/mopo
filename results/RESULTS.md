# PaCE — synthetic validation results

Generated by `experiments/render_results.py` from `synthetic_results.json`. 
Do not edit by hand.

**Setup.** 20 seeds per cell, 600 steps, 19,200 rollouts per run — identical budget for every method, including the multi-model baseline. lr=1.0, entropy=0.01, 4 contexts, reward noise 0.05. Hypervolume is measured against a shared reference point with greedy decoding; `±` is the standard deviation across seeds.

**What this validates.** The optimizer's frontier behaviour on a synthetic multi-objective bandit. It says nothing about language models — no LLM has been trained with this.

## 4. Coverage bandit

The bandit can only pay off when there is something to reallocate: frontier regions of genuinely uneven difficulty, *and* a direction grid larger than the group size. With `D` close to `K` and sampling without replacement, a group covers nearly the whole grid whatever the bandit prefers, so the mechanism is inert by construction.

## Reading these numbers

Standard errors are `std / sqrt(20)`, so a gap of roughly 0.031 hypervolume is about two standard errors. Several gaps below are smaller than that and are called out as such.


**1. The headline, with its condition attached.** On the concave front, at identical rollout budget, `pace` reaches 58% of oracle with **one** model while `fixed_scalar` — the status quo of N independent fixed-weight runs — reaches 27% with **nine**. That is the result the method was built for.


On the convex front the ordering reverses: `fixed_scalar` reaches 94% and `pace` 71%. This is not a wash to be explained away — when the front is convex, weighted sums are the right tool and PaCE's machinery is overhead. **PaCE's advantage is conditional on front geometry, and it costs something when the geometry is benign.** If you know your objectives trade off smoothly, use the simple thing.


**2. Scale invariance is the most robust result here.** Applying `exp(4x)` to one objective does not move the Pareto set by a single point. `pace_uniform` is unmoved by it (0.2620 -> 0.2620, identical). `cond_linear` collapses from 0.2378 to 0.0460 — and note the ± of 0.0000: every seed fails the same way, so this is a deterministic structural failure, not bad luck. MO-GRPO-style per-objective z-scoring (0.2215 -> 0.2076) survives much better than a raw weighted sum, as its authors intend, but is still not invariant — a z-score is only affine-invariant, and this transform is not affine.


**3. Frontier shaping is the component with the clearest evidence.** Removing it (`lambda_front=0.0`) costs 11.7 points of oracle (48.3% vs 60.0%), several standard errors. Removing just the crowding half costs 6.6 points. Doubling it to 0.6 changes nothing, so the setting is not delicate.


**4. Two mechanisms are *not* established by this experiment, and saying so is more useful than a hedge.**


- *The cross-direction leave-one-out baseline.* Collapsing it to a constant baseline (`loo_shrinkage=1.0`) costs only 1.9 points, within noise. That is not evidence against it so much as evidence that this environment cannot test it: the action space is tiny and the gradient variance is already low, which is precisely where a variance-reduction device has the least to offer. It needs a setting with expensive, high-variance rollouts — an LLM — to be worth anything or to be shown worthless.
- *The coverage bandit.* Its effects are consistently within one standard error. The direction is at least consistent with the mechanism: it helps at `D=41` and not at `D=9`, matching the prediction that a group sampling 8 of 9 grid directions without replacement covers the grid regardless of what the bandit prefers. But 'directionally consistent and inside the noise' is not a result.


**5. Controllability catches a failure every other metric misses.** `cond_dominance` posts a competitive 58.8% hypervolume on the concave front — near the top of the table — with a controllability of +0.072. Its preference knob does essentially nothing. A Pareto-rank-only advantage is direction-agnostic by construction, so the policy has no reason to learn what `w` means; it finds a good frontier and refuses to be steered along it. Judged on hypervolume alone it would look like one of the better methods here.


### Caveats

- One synthetic environment with a linear policy over ~21 actions. The policy class is provably expressive enough to cover the front, so failures are attributable to the objective rather than to capacity — but that is a long way from a language model.
- Hyperparameters (lr, entropy, grid resolution) were selected by sweeping `pace_uniform`, then applied unchanged to every method. That is a mild advantage to PaCE and the reader should discount accordingly.
- Absolute numbers sit near 60% of oracle; none of these runs is trained to convergence, and the seed-to-seed spread is wide.
- **No language model has been trained with this.** Nothing here transfers automatically.


## Method key

- `pace` — Full PaCE: Tchebycheff + cross-direction LOO + frontier shaping + coverage bandit
- `pace_uniform` — PaCE with uniform direction sampling (ablates the coverage bandit)
- `pace_no_front` — PaCE with lambda_front=0 (ablates dominance + crowding shaping)
- `pace_linear` — PaCE with linear scalarization (ablates Tchebycheff)
- `cond_linear` — Homogeneous groups, weighted-sum advantage (conditioned GRPO as usually applied)
- `cond_mognorm` — Homogeneous groups, MO-GRPO-style per-objective z-score then sum
- `cond_dominance` — Homogeneous groups, Pareto-rank-only advantage (PRPO-flavoured)
- `fixed_scalar` — N independent runs at fixed weights, equal total budget (status quo)
